# Independent blind visual review — #139 rig slice

**Verdict: needs visual fixes before acceptance.** The candidate demonstrates a coherent, turning 3D character with articulated motion and visible lighting changes. It does not yet convincingly achieve the requested Animal Crossing GameCube/DS museum experience. Foot contact is still weak at the actual embedded viewing size, and the camera hides too much of the character's face and gait. This is a visual verdict, not a finding about the rig implementation or its automated checks.

## Evidence and method

Reviewed the supplied PNG captures at their stored native dimensions, including `entry`, `warm`, `cool`, `disabled`, `white-far`, `head-look`, `hand-interact`, `sprite`, and `original`, plus `white-room`, `white-arch`, `gallery-light-mid`, `gallery-light-far`, and `warm-isolated`. Paths below are relative to `docs/evidence/gallery-rigged-visitor/` unless stated otherwise.

Decoded `motion.webm`: 1600 × 900, 208 frames, timestamps 0.033–6.933 seconds, regularly spaced at approximately 30 fps. Inspected full-frame samples across the clip and consecutive frames 0–17, 30–47, and 135–152. Consecutive-frame sheets were assembled in `/tmp/astra-rig-blind/` from native, unscaled 260 × 200 crops at image coordinate (710, 330). These are inspection aids, not new project deliverables. Frame numbering here starts at zero.

Compared the supplied house reference, blue museum reference, owner's reported-foot-gap screenshot, and the preserved `reference-5730-5734.png`, `reference-moving-5900.png`, and `reference-contact.png` sheets in `docs/research/gallery-blind-reference-gap-review/`. The saved reference sheets were viewed as supplied; their original video was not available in the listed folder. No implementation, logs, existing review prose, or other agents' conclusions were inspected. No generation or spending occurred.

## Ranked findings

1. **High — Foot contact remains visually weak, although the previous obvious gap is reduced.** In `warm.png`, examine the boots and floor around x=520–640, y=540–605. The small dark area beneath the torso gives some grounding, but neither boot has a clearly readable contact patch at its sole. Their brown color also merges into the parquet. In native `entry.png`, the same issue compresses into the area around x=810–865, y=470–500: the feet are difficult to distinguish from the floor, so placement feels uncertain. `white-room.png`, around x=525–625, y=590–625, does reveal a faint central shadow; it is not correct to say the character has no shadow. It still gives less convincing individual-foot support than the supplied GameCube reference's readable feet and attached floor shadow. The owner's earlier conspicuous separation between feet and dark shadow is not reproduced as clearly here. **Fix target:** keep the existing improvement, but make the supporting foot and attached shadow legible at the embedded size throughout a stride. The captures do not establish a specific mesh-height error.

2. **High — The steep camera conceals the gait and face.** In `white-room.png`, most of the visible character is the back/top of the head, with the legs reduced to two tiny projections beneath the belt. This persists in `motion.webm` frames 135–152 (4.533–5.100 s); arms are readable, but foot support is not. Even the front-facing `warm.png` emphasizes the hair cap, while the facial features sit in a narrow lower strip. The house reference exposes more face/body and a readable stepping foot; the blue museum reference also gives substantially more frontal leg/body information. **Fix target:** judge and tune the camera presentation with the character facing forward, sideways, and away so a viewer can see the support-foot change. This is principally a view-angle/readability issue. Raw character size is plausible: in `entry.png` the character occupies roughly one-third of the inner scene's height, similar to the supplied house composition. Enlarging the character alone would not resolve it.

3. **Medium — Lighting response is real, but the intended local-light experience is only partly demonstrated.** `warm.png` versus `disabled.png` changes the character from shaded colored volume to black silhouette at the same framing. `white-room.png` is visibly brighter than the gallery captures. These are positive visual evidence that the new character participates in lighting. However, `warm.png` and `cool.png` both read predominantly brown/orange on the head, skin, and boots; the named cool condition does not produce an unmistakably cool character to the eye. Their surroundings/view differ, so this comparison cannot isolate the cause. The blue museum reference has a cooler, more restrained room-wide balance than this warm parquet scene. **Fix target:** capture one continuous, repeatable walk through a visibly warm and cool region with the same view and inspect head, shirt, and floor together. Do not equate the black disabled control with proof that the final local-light balance is right. The room's broader palette is an art-direction issue separate from the rig.

4. **Medium — Character likeness has moved toward a generic fantasy figure.** In `entry.png`, `warm.png`, and `hand-interact.png`, the large brown hair mass, green tunic, broad belt, pouch, and brown gloves/boots read as a small fantasy adventurer. The silhouette is round and approachable, but the larger eyes, simpler contemporary clothing, and expressive face of the supplied reference language are less apparent. `sprite.png` carries more immediate Animal Crossing-like facial/clothing cues, while the rig carries more convincing volume. Those are separate merits. **Fix target:** retain the 3D behavior while bringing the character's visible face, clothing, and proportions closer to the intended visitor design. A rig prototype can use this figure as a stand-in; it should not be called finished visual likeness.

5. **Medium — Smoothness is promising; no-lag/no-crawl acceptance is not established by this capture.** Consecutive frames 0–17 (0.033–0.600 s) show intermediate rotation and arm/foot poses through the turn, rather than a flat sprite suddenly flipping direction. Frames 30–47 (1.033–1.600 s) show changing limb poses. I found no obvious gross frozen interval or size pop in the inspected motion samples. Nevertheless, the camera follows/orbits, the feet are small, and no input timing is visible. The nearly constant pose in frames 135–152 occurs while the room moves in the view; that can be camera motion around a standing character and is not sufficient evidence of foot sliding. A regular 30 fps recording is also not a measurement of application responsiveness. **Verification target:** record visible key-down/key-up, a straight traverse, immediate reversal, and stop, with floor landmarks and soles readable. This is an evidence gap, not a substantiated claim that the implementation currently lags.

## What already holds

The figure reads as a volume across front, side, and back views. The turn sequence retains coherent anatomy. The hand interaction capture visibly changes the pose without an obvious detached limb or broken silhouette. Lighting changes affect the character, and apparent character size stays broadly stable throughout the sampled motion. I would preserve those gains.

`head-look.png` differs too subtly from the neutral capture to establish a convincing look gesture at the embedded size; it does not by itself prove a broken head rig. This should be shown as a short visible action if head looking is part of acceptance.

## Acceptance decision

**Do not sign off #139 as visually complete on this evidence.** First make contact and gait readable with the final camera, then capture a short controlled motion/light pass. Technical rig progress is visible and worth keeping. Animal Crossing likeness, physical grounding, and proof of responsive motion remain distinct acceptance questions; passing one does not settle the others.
